MINT OREO Linux Mini PC
Hardware Repurposing, Linux & Custom FDM Enclosure

A compact Linux entertainment computer built by repurposing hardware from an aging HP laptop, optimizing the system for 1080p media playback, and designing a custom 3D-printed enclosure from scratch to serve as a dedicated bedside YouTube station.

Project Overview

Giving Old Hardware a New Purpose

MINT OREO began as an experiment to determine whether functional hardware from an aging laptop could be given a useful second life instead of being discarded.

Solving a Real Everyday Problem

The larger motivation came from a small but repeated inconvenience. Before this project, I used my main modern laptop as a temporary media station by connecting it through HDMI to the TV near my bed for YouTube playback at night.

As a college student, that laptop was also the computer I carried back and forth in my backpack. This meant repeatedly unplugging and reconnecting the HDMI cable, worrying about long-term wear on the laptop's HDMI port, and occasionally getting ready for bed only to realize the laptop was still packed away and not connected to the TV. The goal of MINT OREO was to replace that temporary setup with a dedicated, always-available media computer.

The project initially centered around the motherboard from the first laptop I ever owned, an older HP system using an AMD processor. I removed the motherboard from its original chassis, evaluated its thermal performance, experimented with Linux, and began planning a custom enclosure that could convert the laptop hardware into a desktop-style mini PC.

The AMD platform from my first laptop was the original basis for the project.

Early bench testing before the system was integrated into a dedicated enclosure.

Testing eventually showed that the AMD hardware was not well suited to the intended workload. A second motherboard from my grandmother's former HP Pavilion 17 became available and provided substantially better thermal and media-playback performance.

The project therefore evolved beyond simply rehousing a laptop motherboard. It became a process of comparing two aging computing platforms, selecting hardware based on real-world performance, troubleshooting standalone laptop operation, configuring Linux, and designing an entirely new mechanical enclosure around the final system.

Hardware Selection

AMD Platform - Original Concept

The original AMD motherboard remained functional, but thermal testing revealed temperatures that were too high for a system intended for extended media playback.

Intel Platform - Final Selection

The HP Pavilion 17 motherboard used an Intel Core i3-3110M with Intel HD Graphics 4000. It provided substantially lower operating temperatures and a noticeably smoother YouTube experience.

Workload Original AMD Board Intel Core i3 Board
Idle ~75 °C ~50 °C
YouTube Playback ~90-100 °C ~60-70 °C

Engineering Decision: Rather than continuing to optimize a platform that was poorly suited to the intended workload, I changed the final hardware platform. The Intel system produced a much larger improvement in temperature and media performance than further modification of the original AMD system.

Cooling System Service

Before enclosure design began, the cooling system of the Intel board was serviced. The original laptop blower fan and heatsink were cleaned, and the aging thermal compound was replaced with fresh thermal paste.

The original HP heat pipe and blower assembly was retained in the finished system. Because the Intel platform operated at much lower temperatures than the AMD board, the factory cooling system remained effective once it had been cleaned and repasted.

External Display Troubleshooting

A Laptop Motherboard Without a Laptop Display

One of the largest challenges in converting the HP Pavilion 17 into a standalone mini PC was external display behavior.

The motherboard was designed around its original internal LCD panel. Although HDMI could function after the operating system loaded, the board would not display POST or BIOS screens over HDMI when the internal laptop display was disconnected.

Known Limitation: Normal Linux operation uses the external HDMI display, but accessing POST or BIOS configuration still requires connecting the original internal laptop display interface.

Windows Driver Investigation

Windows was initially used as a diagnostic environment while I determined whether the external-display problems were caused by hardware, firmware, or graphics configuration.

The graphics hardware was initially recognized only as Microsoft Basic Display Adapter.

Installing the correct Intel graphics driver.

Successful recognition of Intel HD Graphics 4000.

Correct driver installation confirmed that the graphics hardware and HDMI interface were functional, helping isolate the remaining behavior to system configuration and firmware limitations.

BIOS Testing

BIOS boot settings, UEFI configuration, legacy support, and other startup options were investigated while attempting to obtain external video before the operating system loaded. The limitation remained, even though HDMI worked after startup.

Linux Migration

Linux Mint XFCE 22.3

The completed computer uses Linux Mint XFCE 22.3. XFCE was selected for its relatively lightweight desktop environment, allowing more of the available hardware resources to be dedicated to browser and video playback.

Early Linux testing was not completely straightforward. Compatibility boot options were explored through GRUB, and Windows had left the existing NTFS filesystem in a hibernated state that Linux refused to mount read-write.

GRUB compatibility troubleshooting.

NTFS mount failure caused by Windows hibernation.

Successful Linux Mint boot.

Getting HDMI Working Under Linux

After Linux Mint was installed, the external monitor appeared in the system's display configuration and was detected through xrandr, but initially produced no visible image.

The terminal output confirmed that HDMI was physically connected and that Linux recognized the external display at 1920 x 1080. This helped show that the issue was not simply a failed connector or undetected monitor.

The breakthrough came from disabling XFCE display compositing. After compositing was disabled, the HDMI display began operating normally. The fix continued working after reboot, and the original LVDS laptop display could then be disabled within Linux.

Final Display Behavior: The system now boots into Linux and operates entirely from an external HDMI display during normal use. The original laptop panel is only required when direct BIOS/POST access is needed.

Media Playback Optimization

Getting the computer to boot successfully was only part of the goal. The system needed to be convenient enough to replace the laptop I had been repeatedly connecting to my TV, and capable enough that using it for nightly YouTube playback would not feel like a compromise.

Browser behavior, hardware acceleration, video codec selection, display settings, and desktop compositing were evaluated specifically around YouTube playback on the older Intel hardware.

With the final Linux and browser configuration, the system can provide stable 1080p YouTube playback with almost no dropped frames. This became the performance target that defined the final system as successful.

Custom Enclosure Design

Designed from Scratch in Onshape

Once the electronics and software configuration were sufficiently stable, I designed an entirely new enclosure in Onshape around the HP Pavilion 17 motherboard.

Unlike standardized desktop hardware, the laptop motherboard has an irregular outline, proprietary mounting locations, distributed I/O, ribbon-connected daughterboards, and cooling hardware originally designed around the laptop chassis.

The enclosure was therefore built around the motherboard itself as the primary mechanical constraint. Dedicated mounting points, ventilation openings, port cutouts, removable panels, and internal component clearances were incorporated directly into the CAD model.

Motherboard Layout & Fit Testing

Accurately transferring the motherboard mounting pattern into CAD was one of the most important parts of the enclosure design.

Rather than repeatedly printing complete enclosure sections, I created several simplified and skeletonized test prints containing the geometry needed to verify mounting-hole locations and component clearances.

This allowed the motherboard standoff layout to be refined while using less filament and reducing print time before committing to the complete enclosure.

Physical fit testing of the motherboard and SSD against the printed base.

Internal mounting geometry after the standoff layout was established.

Modular Component Mounting

Adapting Instead of Reprinting

Once the primary enclosure was printed, several smaller mounting features either required adjustment or were damaged during assembly. Instead of discarding the completed enclosure and printing an entirely new version, I designed small screw-mounted repair and retention pieces.

The 128 GB 2.5-inch SATA SSD was one example. A small printed retention component was added using the drive's existing mounting points, allowing it to be secured without redesigning the complete enclosure.

Design Approach: When a localized feature needed improvement, a small replaceable component was generally preferable to reprinting the entire chassis. This reduced material waste, print time, and unnecessary redesign.

Original Power Button Integration

The original laptop power-switch PCB was retained rather than modifying the motherboard's electrical power-control circuitry.

Exploded CAD view of the custom button and its retaining plate + PCB-retaining plate.

The completed external button installed in the top panel.

A printed bracket secures the original power-button PCB inside the enclosure. A separate printed actuator passes through the top panel and mechanically presses the original tactile switch.

This preserved the manufacturer's original electrical interface while adapting its mechanical operation to the new chassis.

Threaded Inserts & Serviceability

Brass heat-set threaded inserts were incorporated into the printed enclosure to provide durable reusable threads at major fastening points.

The inserts allow the enclosure to be opened and reassembled for maintenance without repeatedly driving screws directly into PLA. This was particularly useful for a project expected to continue receiving hardware and software adjustments.

FDM Fabrication

The enclosure was printed on a Bambu Lab A1 Mini using a 0.4 mm nozzle. The structure was divided into multiple printable pieces that could be fastened together around the internal hardware.

Two filament styles were intentionally combined:

  • Black PLA for the upper and lower enclosure sections
  • Blue/silver silk PLA for the center body

Black PLA enclosure panels.

Blue/silver silk PLA center sections with threaded inserts.

Print Failure & Recovery

One large enclosure print failed after the printer nozzle clogged and stopped extruding filament. The failed nozzle was replaced, and the component was successfully reprinted.

The failure was relatively simple to correct, but it reinforced the practical manufacturing side of the project: a finished CAD model does not guarantee a successful physical part.

Final Assembly

The finished internal layout integrates the HP Pavilion motherboard, 12 GB of RAM, 128 GB SATA SSD, original cooling hardware, Wi-Fi, power-switch PCB, and required daughterboard connections inside the custom enclosure.

Results & Tradeoffs

Outcomes

  • Created a dedicated bedside media station for TV-connected YouTube playback
  • Repurposed aging laptop hardware into a functional standalone computer
  • Reduced YouTube operating temperatures from the original platform's ~90-100 °C to approximately 60-70 °C
  • Achieved stable 1080p YouTube playback with almost no dropped frames
  • Configured Linux Mint XFCE 22.3 for older hardware
  • Resolved external HDMI operation under Linux
  • Designed a complete enclosure from scratch in Onshape
  • Integrated laptop motherboard, storage, cooling, controls, and I/O into a serviceable enclosure
  • Reused the original laptop power-switch electronics instead of electrically modifying the board
  • Used modular printed repairs to avoid unnecessary enclosure reprints

Known Tradeoffs

  • BIOS and POST output still require the original internal laptop display connection
  • The enclosure is larger than a purpose-built modern mini PC because it follows the irregular geometry of a laptop motherboard
  • Bluetooth requires a USB adapter
  • The system is optimized for lightweight entertainment rather than modern high-performance computing

Engineering Takeaways

Useful Projects Often Start With Repeated Friction

The project was not motivated only by reusing old hardware. It also came from a repeated daily annoyance: moving my main laptop between school use and TV-connected media use. Building a dedicated device turned that inconvenience into a practical system-integration problem.

Hardware Selection Should Follow the Workload

The original AMD board was functional, but its thermal behavior made it poorly suited to extended YouTube playback. Changing platforms produced a much larger improvement than continuing to optimize unsuitable hardware.

Prototype Only What Needs Testing

Skeletonized mounting-layout prints allowed critical motherboard dimensions to be tested without repeatedly printing complete enclosure sections.

Repair Can Be Better Than Redesign

When individual mounting features needed revision, small add-on parts allowed the existing enclosure to remain in service instead of wasting an otherwise successful large print.

Old Hardware Can Still Be Useful

The Core i3-3110M is far behind modern processors, but matching the hardware with an appropriate operating system and workload allowed it to remain useful for reliable 1080p media playback.

System Integration Crosses Disciplines

The completed computer required mechanical design, computer hardware, thermal management, Linux configuration, graphics troubleshooting, and additive manufacturing to work together as one system.

Final Result

MINT OREO now operates as a standalone Linux mini PC capable of reliable 1080p YouTube playback while giving aging laptop hardware a useful second life. More importantly, it now fills the role that previously required repeatedly moving my main laptop between school use and TV-connected media use.

The project began with the motherboard from my first laptop and was eventually completed using my grandmother's former HP Pavilion 17, which offered the stronger thermal and media-performance characteristics needed for the final system.

What started as an attempt to reuse an old motherboard ultimately became a system-level project involving hardware evaluation, thermal testing, operating-system configuration, display troubleshooting, CAD, additive manufacturing, and mechanical integration.
